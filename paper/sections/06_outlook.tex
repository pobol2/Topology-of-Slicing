\section{Outlook}
\label{main:outlook}

The two applications demonstrate the framework; they do not exhaust it. Each was obtained by the same steps: read a slicer link by link, find a choice the field holds fixed, change it, and let the composition rules state what the new construction owes. We see three directions in which the same procedure extends.

\paragraph{Unexplored link choices.}
Table~\ref{tab:main-families} is sparse. Every reviewed family builds geometry first and treats the process $\Theta$ as fixed, and the two constructions of this paper fill only two new cells. Other choices remain open. Sources can lie between a stack of layers and one volume, for instance a few connected pieces chosen to match the part and the machine at once. Traversals of a connected source need not be Hilbert curves, and other curves trade locality, smoothness, and turning differently. Construction orders can interleave the links rather than fix one first. A process-aware order, for example, could let thermal or curing constraints in $\Theta$ shape the source split as the posture sheets did here. Each of these choices comes with its own obligations, and the composition rules say in advance what they are.

\paragraph{Lower bounds that combine several links.}
The two bounds of this paper each count components at one link. In a real slicer, obstructions arise at several links at once: a part decomposed into $k$ near-convex pieces, printed by a robot whose safe postures form $c$ sheets. Treated separately, they give at least $k-1$ transitions and $c-1$ switches. A plan with no extra interruptions would have to align the source pieces with the sheets. When they do not align, we expect the true minimum to be counted by the components of a combined space, consisting of pairs of source pieces and sheets that a continuous stretch can occupy together, and to exceed both separate bounds. Stating and proving such bounds, and building sources that attain them, would turn the counting argument from a diagnosis of one link into a design rule for the whole chain.

\paragraph{Uses beyond deposition.}
None of these steps is specific to deposition. Any process that covers a region with a prescribed measure along a path executed by a machine has the same chain. Examples are machining, where the measure is material removed; inspection, where it is sensor dwell; and spraying and welding, where it is coating or heat input. Each has its own forced interruptions, such as tool retractions, re-fixturing, and torch restarts, and its own machine with posture sheets. We expect the remaining unexplored link choices and orders, and lower bounds that combine obstructions from several links, to be the next uses of the framework, within slicing and beyond it.
