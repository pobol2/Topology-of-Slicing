\section{First application: changing the source}
\label{main:construction}

The first application replaces the source and lets the composition rules state what the remaining links must supply. A disconnected source forces transitions (Section~\ref{main:source}); choosing the unit cube $\Xs=[0,1]^3$, a connected volume, removes that structural cause. The tool path ${\vect{x}}=\Phi\circ{\vect{\upxi}}$ is continuous if both the traversal and the geometry map are, and by \eqref{eq:main-measure} it has the prescribed occupation measure if
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-volume-obligations}
  {\vect{\upxi}}_\#\lambda^1=\lambda^3,\qquad \Phi_\#\lambda^3=\mu_\Omega ,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda^3$ is uniform volume on the cube. The traversal must therefore be a continuous, measure-preserving curve through the cube, and the geometry map must be a continuous map that pushes uniform volume onto $\mu_\Omega$. A space-filling curve meets the first obligation exactly, and optimal transport meets the second, with continuity under the convexity assumptions below. The resulting \emph{transported Hilbert path}, or transported path for short, gives each map a single job: the traversal orders, and the geometry map shapes. The construction ends at the tool path: the lift and the process remain abstract links of the chain.

\subsection{Construction: Hilbert orders, transport shapes}
The traversal ${\vect{\upxi}}\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]^3$ is the three-dimensional Hilbert curve \cite{hilbert1891,sagan1994,bader2013}. It visits every point of the cube continuously, spends equal time in equal volumes, and keeps points visited at nearby times close together. At a finite order $m$ it visits the centres ${\vect{u}}_1,\dots,{\vect{u}}_n\in[0,1]^3$ of $n=8^m$ cells, consecutive cells sharing a face \cite{haverkort2017}; Appendix~\ref{sec:vsfc-hilbert} establishes its measure preservation, locality, and finite-order estimates. The geometry map $\Phi\colon[0,1]^3\to\Omega$ is the optimal-transport map: among all maps that send uniform volume in the cube to the target distribution $\mu_\Omega$, it minimises the mean squared displacement \cite{brenier1991,peyre2019computational}.

Composition gives ${\vect{x}}_\#\lambda^1=\Phi{}_\#\lambda^3=\mu_\Omega$; when transport is continuous, the composed path is continuous as well. Figure~\ref{fig:hilbert-transport} illustrates the construction numerically in two dimensions, and Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} (bottom) on the Stanford bunny. Its distinction is to transport a traversed volume while preserving the prescribed occupation measure: earlier manufacturing uses of Hilbert curves traverse layers or deposition surfaces \cite{bertoldi1998domain,papacharalampopoulos2018path}, while curved-layer, multi-axis, and neural slicers deform layers \cite{etienne2019curvi,zhang2022s3,liu2024neural}.

\subsection{Guarantees on convex parts}
\label{main:convex}
The Hilbert curve is continuous, so the transported path is continuous wherever the transport map is. Optimal transport always delivers the prescribed distribution, but it need not be continuous: a map that sends a cube onto a shape with a hole may have to tear. Convex targets avoid this. If the part is compact and convex with nonempty interior, and the target density is bounded above and below by positive constants, the transport map is H\"older continuous on the closed cube \cite{caffarelli1992,jhaveri2022regularity}; Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-convex-transport} explains why these assumptions suffice. We call these assumptions the \emph{convex setting}. In it, the ideal transported path is continuous and has the prescribed occupation measure, ${\vect{x}}_\#\lambda^1=\mu_\Omega$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:vsfc-guarantees}(ii)): the nozzle never has to break off and travel, and the time it spends in each region matches the prescription. Outside the convex setting, the guarantee is absent rather than refuted: transport still delivers the prescribed distribution, but no theorem makes it continuous.

At a finite Hilbert order $m$, the guarantee becomes a bound. Waypoints $\hat {\vect{x}}_i\in\mathbb R^3$ that lie within a distance $\varepsilon$ of $\Phi({\vect{u}}_i)$ satisfy (Theorem~\ref{thm:vsfc-guarantees}(i))
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-bounds}
  \text{covering radius}\leq C_\Phi2^{-\beta m}+\varepsilon,\qquad
  \text{step length}\leq C_\Phi2^{-\beta m}+2\varepsilon ,
\end{equation}
where $\beta\in(0,1]$ and $C_\Phi>0$ are the H\"older exponent and constant of $\Phi$, the covering radius is the largest distance from a point of $\Omega$ to its nearest waypoint, and the step length is the largest distance between consecutive waypoints. Refinement shrinks the first term; only a more accurate geometry map shrinks the second. Neither $C_\Phi$ nor $\varepsilon$ is known for a computed map, so the computation below supplies a bound of its own.

\subsection{Implementation: semi-discrete transport}
\label{main:semidiscrete}
The waypoints are computed by semi-discrete optimal transport between the part and the finite Hilbert curve \cite{aurenhammer1998minkowski,merigot2011multiscale,levy2015numerical}. Each site ${\vect{u}}_i$ of the order-$m$ curve carries mass $1/n$, a deposition-time share of $1/n$, and the part carries the continuous target $\mu_\Omega=\rho_\Omega\,\mathrm{d}{\vect{y}}$, whose density $\rho_\Omega$ may be graded. The solver partitions the part into the Laguerre cells $L_i(\Omega;{\vect{\uppsi}})$ of the sites, power cells with weights ${\vect{\uppsi}}\in\mathbb R^n$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:vsfc-semidiscrete}), and adjusts the weights until every cell carries its site's share of the target, up to solver tolerance,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-equal-mass}
  \mu_\Omega\bigl(L_i(\Omega;{\vect{\uppsi}})\bigr)=\tfrac1n\qquad(i=1,\dots,n),
\end{equation}
which makes the map sending each cell $L_i$ to ${\vect{u}}_i$ the optimal transport from $\mu_\Omega$ to the sites \cite{aurenhammer1998minkowski,peyre2019computational}. Each waypoint is the centroid of its cell under $\mu_\Omega$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-centroid}
  \hat {\vect{x}}_i=n\int_{L_i(\Omega;{\vect{\uppsi}})}{\vect{y}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_\Omega({\vect{y}}) ,
\end{equation}
and the centroids are visited in Hilbert order: the computed tool path $\hat{\vect{x}}$ is the polygonal path through $\hat {\vect{x}}_1,\dots,\hat {\vect{x}}_n$. A Hilbert site thus labels a cell of the part, and the waypoint is that cell's centre of mass. On the cube with uniform density, each cell is the Hilbert cell and $\hat {\vect{x}}_i={\vect{u}}_i$: the construction is the identity, as it should be.

This construction controls, cell by cell, what the ideal map is meant to control; Appendix~\ref{sec:vsfc-semidiscrete} proves its distribution, order, and containment properties (Proposition~\ref{prop:sd-mass}) and the hull-gap bounds used below.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Cell mass.} Every waypoint stands for the same mass $1/n$ of the target, matching the equal time that the Hilbert curve spends in each cell. No target sample is drawn and none is deleted.
  \item \emph{A computable distribution bound.} Sending each cell's mass to its own centroid moves no mass farther than the largest cell diameter $d^{(m)}=\max_i\operatorname{diam}L_i(\Omega;{\vect{\uppsi}})$. Unlike $\varepsilon$ in \eqref{eq:main-bounds}, $d^{(m)}$ is measured for every computed path.
  \item \emph{Order.} Each centroid stays power-nearest to its own site, so the waypoints inherit the Hilbert labels without reordering.
\end{itemize}

Three things are not controlled. First, how fast $\hat {\vect{x}}_i$ approaches $\Phi({\vect{u}}_i)$ is not certified, so the step bound in \eqref{eq:main-bounds} remains conditional. Second, equal cell mass is not waypoint-density accuracy: regions thinner than a cell are not resolved (Proposition~\ref{prop:sd-mass}(iii)). Third, equal mass says nothing about containment. On a convex part each clipped cell is convex, so every centroid and every segment between consecutive centroids lies in the part. On a non-convex part, two mechanisms break this: a cell straddling a reentrant corner is clipped into disconnected portions whose combined centroid can lie in the empty region between them, and two cells with centroids inside the part can be joined by a segment that crosses a hole. Figure~\ref{fig:containment-repair} (Section~\ref{main:evidence}) shows both on computed paths.

\subsection{Repair: a volumetric source in several pieces}
\label{main:decomposed}
The chain assigns this failure to the geometry map, not the source: the cube is still connected, and the excursions arise where cells are clipped by holes and reentrant edges. The repair therefore keeps the source volumetric but splits it so that the geometry map's convexity assumption holds, or nearly so. Given a partition of the part into $N$ convex or near-convex pieces $\Omega_1,\dots,\Omega_N\subseteq\Omega$, the source becomes $N$ cubes, one per piece, and the Hilbert traversal and semi-discrete transport are applied independently to each (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, top). Each piece is still filled by a path that runs through its volume, and receives its share $w_j=\mu_\Omega(\Omega_j)$ of deposition time.

How far a piece may be from convex is measured by its \emph{convex-hull gap}, the distance by which its convex hull reaches beyond it,
\[
  \gamma(\Omega_j)=\sup_{{\vect{y}}\in\operatorname{conv}(\Omega_j)}\dist({\vect{y}},\Omega_j).
\]
Centroids lie in the convex hull, and so does the computed path through them, so each piece path leaves its piece by at most $\gamma(\Omega_j)$, with exact containment for convex pieces (Proposition~\ref{prop:sd-hull-gap}). The bound needs no transport regularity, bounds excursion depth only, and concerns the \emph{assigned piece}: an excursion beyond $\Omega_j$ may enter a neighbouring piece and so remain inside the part. Leaving the \emph{whole object}, the failure that decomposition is meant to remove, is excluded only where the region within $\gamma(\Omega_j)$ of a piece lies inside the part.

Decomposition reintroduces separate pieces into the source, so Proposition~\ref{prop:topology-transitions} forces at least $N-1$ transitions between piece paths. Unlike the number of layers, $N$ is set by the partition of the part, not by the Hilbert order, so the count does not grow as the resolution is refined. Sequencing the pieces, and hence executing these transitions, is outside the scope of this paper, and the effect of crossings between neighbouring pieces on local density and overlap is not controlled.
