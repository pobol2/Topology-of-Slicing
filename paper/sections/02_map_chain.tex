\section{The map chain as a slicer design framework}
\label{main:chain}

A conventional slicer bundles several decisions into one algorithm. Separating them lets us compare slicers one decision at a time, trace each limit to the decision that causes it, and construct new slicers by making a decision differently.

\subsection{The map chain}
Figure~\ref{fig:main-chain} reads a slicer as a sequence of decisions. A slicer first chooses a \emph{source} $\Xs$, the space in which the path is designed: a stack of slices, a family of surface patches, or a volume. A \emph{traversal} $\xi$ then decides the order in which the source is visited over normalised deposition time $t\in[0,1]$. A \emph{geometry map} $\Phi$ places each source point in the part $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^3$, which produces the tool path. Finally, a \emph{lift} $\Psi_g$ turns the tool path into motion in the machine's configuration space $\Qs$, and the \emph{process} $\Theta$ turns that motion into the built part $\widehat\Omega$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-chain}
  x(t)=\Phi(\xi(t)),\qquad q=\Psi_g[x],\qquad
  \widehat\Omega=\Theta[q].
\end{equation}
The traversal and the geometry map act point by point. The lift and the process act on whole paths, hence the brackets, because a machine's configuration and the material it has laid down depend on what came before. The subscript $g$ records the usual order: geometry first, machine second.

The chain also decides how much material goes where. Suppose the traversal spends its time in the source according to a distribution $\mu_{\Xs}$, and the geometry map sends $\mu_{\Xs}$ to the prescribed distribution $\mu_\Omega$ on the part. Then the tool path spends its deposition time according to $\mu_\Omega$. Writing $f_\#\mu$ for the image of a distribution $\mu$ under a map $f$, and $\lambda^1$ for uniform time,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-measure}
  \xi_\#\lambda^1=\mu_{\Xs},\quad \Phi_\#\mu_{\Xs}=\mu_\Omega
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  x_\#\lambda^1=\mu_\Omega .
\end{equation}
With constant flow, time shares are material shares. The identity therefore states what it means for a path to cover the part with the right amount of material. Graded density needs no separate link: any chain can carry it through $\mu_{\Xs}$ or $\mu_\Omega$.

A planar slicer illustrates every link. Its source is the set of separate cross-sections $\Xs=\bigsqcup_{k=1}^N(D_k\times\{k\})$. Its traversal zigzags within each slice and visits the slices in order. A gantry with tool offset $o$ then uses
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:main-planar}
  \Phi(u,v,k)=(u,v,z_k),\qquad \Psi_g[x](t)=x(t)-o,
\end{equation}
and the process turns the resulting motion into deposited material. The resulting distribution lives on $N$ slices, so the process must fill the gaps between them. Appendix~\ref{sec:cms-spaces} gives the precise spaces, the deposition-time convention, and the formal definition (Definition~\ref{def:cms}); there, a finite raster's source measure is the occupation measure of the zigzag rather than the ideal area measure on each slice.

The chain is useful because guarantees attach to links. Exact distributions, continuity, containment, and error bounds compose from one link to the next; Appendix~\ref{sec:cms-composition} states the composition inequalities with proofs (Proposition~\ref{prop:composition}) and tabulates the resulting guarantees (Table~\ref{tab:cms-guarantees}). Each failure therefore has an address: a disconnected source forces transitions, a geometry map can tear the path or leave a non-convex part, a lift can switch branches near a singularity \cite{zhang2021singularity}, and the process can leave gaps. This paper stops before the process: it does not model how material is deposited. With this decomposition, each existing method becomes a combination of independently chosen links.

\paragraph{Designing with the chain.}
The same decomposition gives a procedure for deriving new slicers. Read an existing construction link by link and identify a choice that it holds fixed. Then either replace that choice, or change the order in which the links are constructed. Finally, use the composition rules to determine which guarantees survive the change and which become obligations for the remaining links. Sections~\ref{main:construction} and~\ref{main:next} apply this procedure to the two choices that the review below exposes.

\subsection{Existing slicers as link choices}
\label{main:families}
Table~\ref{tab:main-families} places the reviewed slicer families in the chain and can be read in two directions. Along each row, it records which links a family changes relative to the planar chain; this is where the families differ. Down the source column, it records the source as it is actually traversed; this is where families that look very different turn out to agree. Adaptive layer height changes the spacing of the slices. Curved-layer slicers bend them through the geometry map. Multi-axis slicers change the lift, and within-layer continuous paths change the traversal inside each slice. Families often combine changes: S$^3$-Slicer, the scalar-field method, and the neural slicer target multi-axis machines and so also change $\Psi_g$ \cite{zhang2022s3,dai2018support,liu2024neural}.

Two choices remain shared across the reviewed families. All of them build geometry first. And every solid-filling family among them traverses its source as $N$ separate pieces. A volumetric representation does not change this second choice. The trivariate method parameterises a volume but still visits it surface by surface \cite{ezair2018volumetric}, and volumetric frame fields \cite{chermain2025atomizer} and implicit neural fields \cite{dutta2025implicit} likewise describe the solid as a volume but print it as layers or surfaces. Conversely, the many methods that remove travel by a better order, such as Fermat spirals and the continuous planners of wire-arc manufacturing \cite{zhao2016fermat,ding2015practical,diourte2021ctpp}, improve the traversal inside each piece of the source, not the source itself. These shared choices leave two alternatives absent from the reviewed slicing methods: traversing a connected volume, and constructing the machine motion before the tool path. The first concerns the source, whose disconnection carries a cost that no other link can pay back, as the next subsection shows; the second concerns the order of construction (Section~\ref{main:next}).

\subsection{The hidden limit: pieces force stops}
\label{main:source}
Let the source have $N$ separate pieces, each receiving positive deposition time. Any traversal that is continuous except at finitely many times then makes at least $N-1$ \emph{transitions} between pieces, in whatever order it visits them; Appendix~\ref{sec:topology} proves this bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:topology-transitions}) and its consequence for separated images. This is the counting argument of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: a better order shortens the moves but cannot reduce their number. Section~\ref{main:next} applies it again at the machine link.

A transition is an event in the source; its effect on the machine depends on the later links. When the mapped pieces do not touch, a transition becomes a break of the tool path. A break forces a \emph{stop}: a break occupies no deposition time, so the machine crosses it with a travel move during which the flow is switched off. For layers, every transition is a stop, because distinct layers lie at least the layer spacing $h$ apart (Corollary~\ref{cor:topology-propagation}).

The stop count of a layered slicer therefore grows with resolution. A part of height $H$ needs at least $H/h-1$ stops: a 100~mm part has 500 layers and at least 499 stops at a layer spacing of 0.2~mm, and at least 999 stops at 0.1~mm. Disconnected cross-sections add more. No reviewed family in Table~\ref{tab:main-families} lowers this count. Continuous within-layer paths act inside each piece, curved layers and surface patches are still separate pieces, and multi-axis lifts act after the source is fixed. Vase mode escapes with a connected surface, but it prints a single wall \cite{prusaslicer}.

This diagnosis is the first step of the design procedure: since stops originate in the source, the source itself must change. The simplest replacement for a solid is a single connected volume. The composition rules then say what the change costs: a connected source removes forced transitions only if a traversal can visit it continuously and a geometry map can carry it into the part without tearing it apart again. Section~\ref{main:construction} constructs the first algorithm by meeting these two obligations.
