\begin{abstract}
  A slicer bundles several decisions into one algorithm: which source to traverse, in what order, how to place it in the part, how to move the machine, and how to deposit material. Two slicers therefore differ in several decisions at once, and a limit they share can go unnoticed. We describe a slicer as a chain of maps, from a source through the part and the machine to the built part. Each decision becomes a link, and guarantees of coverage, continuity, and containment, together with bounds on implementation error, compose along the chain. The chain gives slicers a common description, traces each guarantee or failure to the link that supplies it, and yields new slicers by changing a link or the order in which the links are built, with the composition rules stating which guarantees survive and which become obligations. We demonstrate the framework on forced interruptions, which are topological: a plan that must visit $c$ path components is interrupted at least $c-1$ times. At the source, every reviewed solid-filling slicer traverses separate layers or surfaces, so its stops grow with resolution. Replacing the source by one connected volume, ordered by a three-dimensional Hilbert curve and carried into the part by optimal transport, gives a continuous path with the prescribed occupation measure on convex parts. In a computational prototype, this path makes no stops on convex parts, where a layered raster's stops roughly double with resolution; on non-convex parts, splitting the source into near-convex pieces trades a fixed number of transitions for bounded excursions. At the machine, the components are the posture sheets of the robot's safe configurations. Reversing the construction order gives kinematics-first slicing, in which the sheets guide the source split: on a three-link planar arm among obstacles, it makes the two sheet switches that the bound requires in a sampled availability model, whereas a geometry-first baseline makes four.
\end{abstract}
