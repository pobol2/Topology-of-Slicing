\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A slicer turns a part into machine motion, and that motion is rarely one continuous stroke. The flow stops between layers, and a robot may have to change posture partway through. Some of these interruptions are chosen by a planner and could be planned away; others are forced by the structure of the slicer, and no planner can remove them. This paper is about the forced ones: where they come from, which stage of the slicer forces them, and how to design a slicer that avoids them. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows a tool path built this way.

On a simple cylinder, a layered raster must stop between layers, and the count roughly doubles each time the resolution doubles (Section~\ref{main:evidence}). Each stop of the material flow can leave a seam, and in some processes a stop is especially costly: it can cut reinforcing fibre in continuous-fibre printing \cite{fang2024exceptional}, cause a restart defect in wire-arc deposition \cite{ding2014toolpath}, or weaken the bond in concrete printing \cite{tay2019timegap}. Better paths within each layer remove some stops, but not those between layers. A robot shows the same pattern one stage later. When obstacles split its safe postures into separate families, some tool paths cannot be followed without a posture reset, however good the inverse kinematics.

\paragraph{Thesis: forced interruptions are topological.}
Both cases have one cause. A continuous stretch of motion stays within one path component of the space it moves in. When the plan must visit $c$ components, it is interrupted at least $c-1$ times, whatever order it uses. Stops come from a source made of separate pieces, the layers. Posture resets come from a fibre space of safe configurations made of separate sheets. The bound counts path components, which is why we call the subject the topology of slicing. Locating an interruption therefore means finding the link that supplies the components. Removing it means changing that link, or the order in which the links are built, and then meeting whatever guarantees the change leaves as obligations.

\paragraph{The map-chain framework.}
We propose to treat a slicer as a chain of maps, from a source representation through the part and the machine to the built part (Section~\ref{main:chain}). Each link carries its own guarantees, which compose. Each forced interruption can therefore be traced to the link that supplies its components, and no change to the other links can remove it. This is also a design method. Reading the reviewed slicers link by link shows which choices they share. Replacing one of those choices, or changing the order in which the links are built, defines a new construction; the chain states which guarantees survive and which become obligations. Within the slicing literature we review, two shared choices force interruptions, at two different links. We construct an algorithm from the alternative to each; the two algorithms are demonstrations of what the framework makes visible, not its full extent.

\paragraph{First application: changing the source.}
Every reviewed solid-filling slicer traverses a source of separate layers or surfaces, including methods that represent the solid as a volume, such as trivariate parameterisations, scalar fields, frame fields, and implicit neural fields \cite{ezair2018volumetric,dai2018support,chermain2025atomizer,dutta2025implicit}: each still cuts the volume into surfaces and visits them in turn. Separating the source from the traversal makes the alternative visible: a source that is one connected volume, traversed directly. The \emph{transported Hilbert path} constructs it (Section~\ref{main:construction}). A three-dimensional Hilbert curve orders a cube, and optimal transport carries the cube into the part. On convex parts the path is continuous and has exactly the prescribed occupation measure, and a semi-discrete computation gives every waypoint an equal share of the target mass, up to solver tolerance. Containment on non-convex parts remains an obligation of the geometry map; decomposing the source into near-convex pieces meets it up to each piece's hull gap. Hilbert curves and optimal transport are not new; the construction is their use as source, traversal, and geometry map. Here the interruption lies in the source, and so does its removal.

\paragraph{Second application: changing the construction order.}
Every reviewed slicer builds the tool path first; robot-aware methods then plan machine motion with that path, or its geometric elements, as input. The path then fixes the posture switches, because it fixes how often the tool passes between regions that only one sheet reaches. Separating the geometry map from the lift makes the alternative visible: build admissible machine motion first and derive the tool path from it through forward kinematics. \emph{Kinematics-first slicing} constructs it (Section~\ref{main:next}). Static feasibility then holds by construction, while coverage and the prescribed deposition density become obligations. The robot's posture sheets guide how the source is split, and patches with continuous safe lifts, each visited once, attain the minimum number of posture switches. The bound is proved by the same counting argument as the bound on stops, now applied at the machine link. Configuration-first coverage is studied in robotics \cite{yang2020coverage}; the framework connects it to the deposition measure and to source decomposition.

\paragraph{Scope of this investigation.}
The two applications are preliminary demonstrations, and each stops at a clearly drawn boundary. Their experimental evidence is computational and differs between them. The volumetric construction is evaluated computationally on five parts and two decompositions (Section~\ref{main:evidence}). It is realised at finite Hilbert order, on polygonal parts, and it stops at the geometric tool path: no lift, machine execution, or deposition is modelled, and no part has been printed. The process costs of stops cited above motivate the example; we do not demonstrate them. The decomposition results are sampled measurements from one experiment, not a proof of containment; the crossings into neighbouring pieces leave local density and overlap unmeasured, and the $k$ piece paths are not yet sequenced. The kinematics-first construction is evaluated on one three-link arm, obstacle layout, and target at Hilbert orders two to four (Section~\ref{sec:kinematics-example}), so one reset against two is not a general rate, and the timings are specific to one machine and implementation. Admissibility there is static: joint rates, dynamics, and the process $\Theta$ remain unchecked. Sheet separation and the optimality of the switch counts hold only in a sampled availability model; sampling rules out neither a connection through an unsampled passage nor availability at unsampled radii. Posture resets are counted, but the physical transition between postures is neither planned nor certified, and no safe physical transition between the two families is claimed. Each of these boundaries is also a point where the chain says what the next step must supply; Section~\ref{main:outlook} returns to them.
