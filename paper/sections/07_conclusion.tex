\section{Conclusion}
\label{main:scope}

The map chain separates the decisions a slicer bundles into one algorithm. Compared link by link, the reviewed families share a source of separate layers or surfaces and a geometry-first order. Because coverage, continuity, containment, and implementation error compose along the chain, each guarantee or failure can be traced to its link. Diagnosis follows: forced interruptions are topological, and the chain shows the link that supplies the components. So does construction: changing that link or the order defines an alternative whose obligations the rules state.

Replacing the source shows that stops growing with resolution, a limit shared by every reviewed solid-filling slicer, can be removed by changing one link and meeting the obligations that the composition rules assign to the others. Changing the construction order shows that the same counting argument governs sheet switches at the machine, and that reversing the order makes static feasibility a guarantee and coverage and density obligations, so that the robot's posture sheets can guide the source split.

These results are computational and, for the robot, established in one sampled example. Maps that stay inside non-convex parts, sequencing of decomposed pieces, and smoothing that preserves containment and respects machine dynamics are the natural next steps, with combined bounds, process-aware construction, and applications beyond deposition as further directions (Section~\ref{main:outlook}). Slicing has been developed by improving paths within a fixed chain. The chain itself---its links, their order, and what each one supplies---is where the limits of a slicer are set, and where new slicers can be designed.
